Drawing on a primary-source study, this article explains why AI projects fail, separates the myths from the real causes and ends with two concrete tools: what an AI maturity assessment checks and how to design a pilot that can reach production.

It's written for executives, general managers, innovation leads and CTOs at mid-sized companies who already ran a proof of concept nobody uses, or who are about to approve an AI budget and want to avoid the same mistake.

## What the data says about AI pilots

The [Lenovo CIO Playbook 2026](https://news.lenovo.com/pressroom/press-releases/research-reveals-ai-is-paying-off-cios-arent-ready/), a study commissioned by Lenovo, with research insights by IDC, reported on January 27, 2026 that 46% of AI proofs of concept had already progressed into production. The fieldwork surveyed 3,120 IT and business decision makers between September and October 2025.

The figure cuts both ways. Nearly half of proofs of concept reach production, and that's a sign of progress. But more than half still haven't. It describes that survey and isn't a forecast for your company. Use it to ask what it takes to get from pilot to stable operation.

## The five real reasons a pilot doesn't reach production

When a pilot stalls, the cause usually sits in one of five areas. Reviewing them before you start costs less than fixing them once the system is built.

### No process and no owner

If nobody can describe who receives the request, who decides and who resolves an exception, automating that workflow reproduces the ambiguity. Name a business owner and map how it runs today before choosing a tool.

### Data that isn't ready or has unclear permissions

A source can exist and still be outdated, have unclear permissions or contradict another. Integration with existing systems can be the first surprise. Test answers with real data and define what the system does when it finds no evidence.

### A team that isn't ready to run it

The person who does the work today knows exceptions that don't show up in the diagram. Bring that person into the pilot and record where they correct or step in. Training has to cover when to trust, review or escalate an output.

### No agreed metric

“Saving time” isn't enough. Pick an observable measure, such as time to a useful answer, cases resolved or hours of manual data entry, take a baseline and agree on who tracks it.

### Open scope, no exit criterion

A pilot that tries to cover every channel and system makes it impossible to tell what worked. Pick one repeated request, one reliable source and one low-risk action, and define in advance when you scale, adjust or stop.

## What an AI maturity assessment is and what it checks

An AI maturity assessment evaluates what the company can absorb before choosing a tool. In the method Data Voices publishes, the first stage reviews “Which processes, which data, which permissions.” Then comes team preparation: “Training for the role that will operate it, not a generic seminar.”

In practice, the assessment looks at five things: the candidate processes, the available data and permissions, the systems it has to integrate with, who will operate the solution and which metric is supposed to move. Its output is a recommendation on which case to test, what's missing, and when it isn't worth moving forward yet.

If you're also choosing who to work with, [what an AI consulting firm's initial assessment should include](/en/blog/how-to-choose-ai-consulting-firm) covers the questions to compare proposals.

## Is my data ready for AI?

Your data is ready for a pilot when you know who updates the source, which version to check and who can see it. It doesn't have to be perfect, just reliable enough to test the system's answers against real cases.

Warning signs: two documents that say different things, permissions nobody manages, undated files or information only one person knows how to interpret. If any of them shows up, the first deliverable can be cleaning up that source. You don't need to migrate everything: you pick one process and one source.

A system for [AI applied to your processes and data](/en/servicios/ia-sobre-datos) has to answer with its source cited, respect permissions, log usage and say “I don't know” when it has no evidence.

## How to design a pilot that can reach production

A pilot that can reach production meets conditions set before anyone writes the first line of code. Require these six:

- One process and one data source, not several at once.
- A closed scope: what gets built, what doesn't and in how many weeks.
- A person or a team who will operate it.
- A metric defined from day one.
- A decision criterion at the end: scale, adjust or stop.
- Budget and owners for the follow-up.

The length depends on the scope, so there's no single number. What you can set is the end date of the closed scope, agreed in writing before you start. Without follow-up after launch, a first version can quietly fall out of use as soon as the data or the team changes.

## How to measure the return on an AI project

To measure the return on an AI project, measure adoption first and then impact, when it can be measured. That's what Data Voices publishes in its “Report to whoever's paying” stage: “Adoption and, when measurable, impact.”

Useful metrics are concrete: response time, hours of manual data entry, cases resolved without intervention and actual use by the team. Take a baseline before the pilot and include supervision hours, integrations and maintenance in the cost. If a financial return can't be estimated yet, report what was observed and which data point is missing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do AI projects fail in companies?

They usually stall when the process has no owner, the data isn't ready, the team that has to use the system wasn't involved, there's no agreed metric or the pilot tried to cover too much. The model can fail too, but evaluating only the model leaves out the conditions needed to operate it.

### Why do AI pilots fail to reach production?

Because a demo proves the idea is possible, and production requires users, permissions, support, a reliable source and someone accountable. When any of those is missing, the pilot stays an isolated test. That's why it pays to define from the start who operates it and what decision gets made when it ends.

### What percentage of AI proofs of concept reach production?

In the Lenovo CIO Playbook 2026, commissioned by Lenovo, with research insights by IDC, and published on January 27, 2026, 46% of AI proofs of concept had already progressed into production, according to a survey of 3,120 IT and business decision makers. Use the figure as a reference, not as a forecast for your company.

### Is my data ready for AI?

It's ready for a pilot if you know who updates the source, which version to check and who can see it. You don't need to migrate everything first: you pick one process and one source, and you clean up only that set. Expanding the scope comes later, once the case has shown value.

### What is an AI maturity assessment?

It's an evaluation of what the company can absorb before choosing a tool: candidate processes, data and permissions, the systems to integrate with, team readiness and the metric you want to move. It ends with a prioritized opportunity and a narrow scope, or with the recommendation to resolve a dependency first.

### How do you design an AI pilot that can go to production?

Define one process and one data source, a closed scope with an end date, who will operate it, a metric from day one and a decision criterion at the end: scale, adjust or stop. Add budget and owners for the follow-up. The length depends on the scope, so there's no universal timeframe.

### How do you measure the return on an AI project?

Measure adoption first: who uses it and for which cases. Then measure the impact you can attribute to the system, such as response time, hours of manual data entry or cases resolved without intervention. Set a baseline before the pilot and add supervision, integrations and maintenance to the cost.

### Why do generative or agentic AI projects fail?

The same five reasons apply as in any AI project: no process owner, data that isn't ready, a team that isn't ready to run it, no agreed metric and an open scope. Those conditions have to be in place before you build, including who will operate the system and when to trust, review or escalate an output.

## Before you invest: review process, data and team

If you already ran a pilot and nobody uses it, the next step is reviewing process, data and team before trying another model. If you haven't started, write the process down on one page and pick one source and one metric.
